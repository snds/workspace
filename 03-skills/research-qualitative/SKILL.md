# Research: Qualitative and Social

Qualitative research explains *how* and *why*; it rarely tells you *how many*. Read it for what it
can claim.

## Field profile

- **Small samples, deep data:** twelve interviews can reveal a mechanism, not a prevalence.
- **Method after the fact:** "we used grounded theory" sometimes labels an unstructured read.
  Look for the actual procedure.
- **Quotes without context:** a vivid quote may be one participant's outlier.
- **Surveys** measure what was asked, how it was asked, of who answered.

## Appraising a qualitative study

1. **Question and fit:** does the method suit the question?
2. **Sampling:** who, how chosen, why that many; saturation claimed and shown?
3. **Analysis:** a named, described procedure (Braun and Clarke's thematic analysis is the most
   cited); coding by more than one person or a stated reason not to.
4. **Reflexivity:** the researchers' position and influence stated.
5. **Reporting:** COREQ's 32 items for interviews and focus groups; missing items hide weaknesses.
6. **Transferability:** the context described well enough to judge whether it applies to yours.

## Surveys

Check the population, the sampling frame, the response rate, the exact wording and order of
questions, and the margin of error. Statistical claims from surveys defer to #15.

## Using it in a synthesis

Qualitative findings support claims about mechanisms, experiences and reasons; they don't support
rates. A Tier 1 claim from qualitative evidence needs three or more independent studies finding the
same theme. Participant research in this workspace's own products goes to [[ux-research-synthesis]].

## Mistakes to avoid

- Reporting "most participants" as a population rate.
- Quoting the most vivid line as typical.
- Accepting a method label without a described procedure.
- Ignoring how a survey question was worded.

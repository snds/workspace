# Research: History and the Humanities

History reasons from what survived, written by people with reasons. The method is reading those
reasons.

## Field profile

- **Survival bias:** archives keep what institutions and the powerful kept. Absence in the archive
  is weak evidence of absence in the past.
- **Primary vs secondary:** a letter from 1850 is primary; a 1990 book about it is secondary. Both
  matter: the primary for what happened, the secondary for how it's been understood.
- **Interpretation is contested:** schools of historiography read the same sources differently.
  Name whose reading you report.
- **Anachronism:** today's terms and categories laid over the past distort it.

## Source criticism

For each document ask:

1. **External:** is it what it claims to be: author, date, place, authenticity, later copies?
2. **Internal:** what did the author know, how close were they to events, what did they want, who
   was the audience?
3. **Corroboration:** do independent witnesses agree? Independent means they didn't share a
   source ([[research-foundations]] §3).
4. **Context:** what else was happening; what words meant then.

## Finding sources

Start from a recent scholarly secondary work for orientation and its bibliography; then library
catalogs, archives' finding aids, digitized collections (national libraries, university archives),
and newspapers of the period. Library guides on using primary sources are a good on-ramp.

## Tracing a quote or an origin story

Most viral history quotes are misattributed or improved. Search the earliest appearance; if the
first instance is decades after the person died, report it as attributed, not said. Origin stories
of terms and practices usually have earlier precedents: search before the claimed first.

## Ethics and citation

The American Historical Association's Statement on Standards of Professional Conduct (updated
2023) sets the discipline's norms on citation, plagiarism and use of evidence. Cite archival items
precisely: repository, collection, box or folder, item, date.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Treating a secondary source's interpretation as the primary fact.
- Quoting a famous line without finding its first appearance.
- Reading modern meanings into old words.
- Taking the archive's silence as proof something didn't happen.
